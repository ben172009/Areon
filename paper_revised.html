Abstract

Academic performance depends not only on students’ cognitive ability and instruction quality but also on the social environment that shapes motivation, participation, and persistence. This mixed-methods study examines the effects of peer interaction, teacher–student relationships, and school social interaction on the academic performance of Grade 9 and 10 students at BeteSeb Academy (Ayertena Branch). Data were collected using structured questionnaires (N = 236 completed responses) and teacher interviews; analyses included descriptive statistics, ANOVA comparisons across achievement groups, and Pearson correlations. Results indicate significant positive associations among peer interaction, teacher interaction, and perceptions of school climate; peer networks and teacher support both related to higher self-reported academic engagement. The paper discusses implications for practice, limitations, and recommendations to strengthen peer-support structures and equitable teacher engagement.

Keywords: Academic performance, Peer interaction, Teacher–student relationship, School social environment, Mixed-methods

Chapter 1 — Introduction

Background of the study

Academic achievement is shaped by more than intelligence and study habits. Research shows that peer relationships, teacher support, and the school social climate influence how students engage with lessons, sustain effort, and perform on assessments. In secondary education, positive peer collaboration can improve motivation and understanding, while negative peer pressure and distraction can reduce academic focus. Likewise, supportive teacher-student relationships foster participation and confidence, while distant or unfair interactions can undermine learner engagement.

This study focuses on Grade 9 and 10 students at BeteSeb Academy in Ayertena Branch, where variations in academic performance suggest that social interaction may be a key factor. By examining peer, teacher, and school social dynamics in this context, the research seeks locally relevant evidence to support improvements in student achievement.

Statement of the problem

There are observable differences in academic achievement at BeteSeb Academy that are not fully explained by instructional or resource differences. This study investigates whether variation in peer and teacher social interactions and the broader school climate help explain differences in academic performance among Grade 9 and 10 students.

Research questions

  1. How do peer relationships influence students’ academic performance at BeteSeb Academy?
  2. What is the relationship between teacher support and students’ academic performance?
  3. What is the combined effect of teacher support and peer interaction on students’ academic performance?

Hypotheses

Three testable hypotheses compare the presence/absence of significant relationships between (1) peer relationships and academic performance, (2) teacher support and academic performance, and (3) the joint effect of peer and teacher interactions on performance.

Objectives

General: To examine the effect of teacher support, peer relationships, and school social interaction on the academic performance of Grade 9–10 students.

Specific: determine relationships between variables, examine combined effects, and produce recommendations.

Chapter 2 — Literature Review

Several theoretical frameworks explain why social interaction matters for learning. Social Constructivism emphasizes that knowledge develops through collaborative dialogue, making peer discussion and group study important learning tools. Self-Determination Theory highlights relatedness as a driver of motivation, suggesting that supportive peers and teachers help students feel valued and engaged. Attachment Theory describes how positive teacher-student relationships provide emotional security, enabling students to take intellectual risks and persist through challenges. Ecological Systems Theory positions the school climate as a broader context that shapes daily student experiences and influences how peer and teacher relationships operate.

Empirical evidence supports these ideas. Peer groups that value academic success tend to raise student motivation and achievement, while negative peer pressure can erode focus. Teacher-student relationships characterized by respect, fairness, and constructive feedback are consistently linked with better engagement and performance. A positive school climate—marked by safety, inclusion, and supportive norms—reinforces these relationships and contributes to stronger academic outcomes.

Chapter 3 — Methodology

Design and approach

Cross-sectional, correlational, mixed-methods design. Primary data from questionnaires and teacher surveys; secondary data from school records.

Population and sampling

Population: 617 Grade 9–10 students. The planned sample using Yamane's formula was 243; the final analysis used 236 completed student responses (response rate ≈ 97.1% of the planned sample). Sample composition used proportionate stratified sampling by gender and academic achievement groups (low, middle, high).

Sample composition (N = 236): Female = 131 (55.5%), Male = 105 (44.5%); Grade 9 = 130 (55.1%), Grade 10 = 106 (44.9%). Age distribution: 14 years = 37 (15.7%), 15 = 83 (35.2%), 16 = 105 (44.5%), 17 = 8 (3.4%), 18 = 3 (1.3%).

Measures and analysis

Questionnaires used Likert-scale items to measure peer interaction, teacher support, school social climate, and academic self-report measures. Where available, GPA records supported the categorization of academic achievement levels. Analyses included descriptive statistics, one-way ANOVA for comparisons across low, middle, and high achievement groups, and Pearson correlation analysis to assess relationships between peer interaction, teacher interaction, and school social interaction. Reliability was confirmed during pilot testing using Cronbach’s alpha, which indicated acceptable internal consistency across survey scales.

Ethical considerations

Participation was voluntary; respondents were informed about the study purpose and confidentiality. Identifiers were not recorded; data were stored securely. Permission to conduct the study was obtained from the school administration and participants gave informed consent.

Chapter 4 — Results and Discussion

The analysis used 236 completed student questionnaires and 12 teacher responses. Descriptive statistics confirmed the demographic profile described earlier. One-way ANOVAs comparing low, middle, and high achievers found statistically significant differences in peer interaction and teacher interaction items, including peer discussion, collaborative learning, teacher feedback, and classroom comfort. Exact F and p values are reported in the tables included with the appendices.

Pearson correlation analysis (N = 236) found significant positive relationships among the main constructs: peer interaction and teacher interaction (r = .421, p < .01), peer interaction and school social interaction (r = .460, p < .01), and teacher interaction and school social interaction (r = .388, p < .01). These moderate correlations indicate that the three constructs are related but represent distinct dimensions of the student experience.

Key interpretations

  • Peer interaction is positively associated with perceived academic benefit (discussion, group study), but also poses distraction risks for some students.
  • Teacher support (respect, feedback, encouragement) is associated with higher perceived academic support and engagement, especially among high achievers.
  • School social environment moderates and reinforces peer and teacher effects.

Chapter 5 — Conclusion and Recommendations

Conclusion

Social interactions within school—peer networks, teacher–student relationships, and school climate—are meaningfully associated with students’ academic engagement and outcomes. Interventions that strengthen structured peer support and ensure equitable teacher engagement may help improve performance, particularly for lower-achieving students.

Recommendations

  • Establish structured peer-support groups and monitored study sessions for low-achieving students.
  • Provide teacher professional development on inclusive classroom interaction and formative feedback.
  • Increase time for guided group discussions and extracurricular clubs that promote positive peer norms.
  • Strengthen guidance and counseling services and monitor equity in teacher support.

Limitations

Cross-sectional design limits causal inference; sample and timeframe restriction reduce generalizability. Responses rely partly on self-report measures, which may introduce social-desirability bias. Future research should consider longitudinal designs, larger multi-school samples, and more objective academic measures.
